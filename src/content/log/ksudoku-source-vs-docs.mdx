When I started grading puzzles in Puzzle Lab, I wanted a shortcut. Difficulty
rating is the hard part of a generator — deciding whether a board is "Medium" or
"Hard" is a judgment call dressed up as a number — and I figured I could borrow
someone else's judgment. KSudoku is open source, mature, and ships with a
difficulty model. Why re-derive it?

So I read the documentation, wrote down the difficulty bands, and started
matching my output to them. The numbers didn't feel right. Puzzles I'd have bet
money were Hard kept landing a tier lower. I assumed my generator was too
generous and started tightening it.

That was the mistake: I trusted the prose over the program.

## What the source actually said

When I finally opened the code instead of the docs, the parameters didn't match.
The documented bands and the values the engine actually used had drifted — the
kind of gap that opens up when the writing happens once and the code keeps
moving. I'd been calibrating against a description of an old version of a thing,
not the thing.

The lesson isn't "KSudoku's docs are bad." It's that **a difficulty number is
only meaningful next to the algorithm that produced it.** A band labelled "Hard"
tells you nothing portable unless you also know exactly which solving techniques
that engine was willing to apply, and in what order. Lift the number out of that
context and it's decoration.

## Why Puzzle Lab grades by technique

This is what pushed me to stop chasing anyone else's scale. Puzzle Lab rates a
puzzle by the **hardest logical technique its solver was forced to use** to reach
a unique solution — naked singles at the easy end, through X-Wing and Y-Wing, up
to the chain-based methods at the top. The `HumanSolver` doesn't guess; if it
can't finish a board by deduction, that board doesn't ship. So the difficulty
label isn't a tuned constant I have to trust — it's a description of the actual
reasoning the puzzle demands.

That has a property the borrowed bands never could: it's honest by construction.
"Expert" means "you will need this specific technique," not "this scored 7.4 on a
scale I can't see." And when I change the solver, the grades change with it,
automatically, because they were never separate from it.

## The takeaway I keep reusing

Read the source, not the summary — especially for anything you're about to build
a calibration on top of. Documentation describes intent; code describes
behaviour; and when they disagree, behaviour wins every time. I lost a couple of
days tightening a generator that was already correct. I'd rather lose an hour
reading a diff.
